## Overview

Large companies have a specific AI problem: not a shortage of tools, but a shortage of *permission that
people can find*. There are approved models, approved prompts, security rules and role-specific
guidance — spread across policy documents that nobody reads at the moment they need them. So people
either don't use AI, or use it in ways nobody sanctioned.

During my internship on Optum's AI-DLC pilot team (June–August 2026) I built an **AI Enablement Agent**:
an internal assistant that answers one question for any employee — *"what is the approved, governed
way for someone in my role to use AI for this?"* — and hands them the pattern, the prompt, the guardrail
and the place to start. It is designed to serve up to 35,000 employees.

## How it works, simply

1. **It knows who is asking.** The agent reads the employee's role and identity from the company's
   systems, so an AI engineer, a data engineer and a product manager asking the same question get
   different, correct answers.
2. **It answers from a curated library, not from the open internet.** Behind it sits a library of
   role-specific *workflows, skills, prompts and safety guardrails* — the governed patterns the
   organisation has actually approved.
3. **The library was built from people, not guesswork.** I interviewed seven kinds of specialist —
   AI/ML engineers, technical programme managers, software engineers, data engineers and others, up to
   six people per role — about how they really work and where AI helped or hurt. Those interviews
   became the role-specific content the agent hands out, and a library structure that new roles and
   teams can be added to.
4. **It's built on the company's own agent framework** (internal "Mesh" agents) in TypeScript, so it
   runs where the rest of the organisation's agents run and inherits their governance.

## The second build: a judge that scores process, not just output

The pilot team ran an internal hackathon on **AI-DLC** — the AI-Driven Development Lifecycle, a way of
working where every change starts as a written plan, is approved, built, checked and closed with a
record. Judging that fairly is hard: the question is not "does the demo work?" but "did the team
actually follow the lifecycle?"

I built a **deterministic Judge LLM** for it: a model that reads each team's repository and record,
scores adherence to the AI-DLC stages against fixed rubrics, and produces consistent, explainable
scores. It ran alongside the human judges as a reliable second opinion — the same submission always
gets the same score, and every score comes with the evidence it was based on.
